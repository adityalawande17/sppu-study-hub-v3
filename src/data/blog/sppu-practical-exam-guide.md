Practicals and lab exams at SPPU are often treated as an afterthought — students focus almost entirely on the theory paper and assume they'll pass practicals by showing up. This is a mistake. Here's how practical assessments actually work and how to prepare properly.

## How Practical Assessment Is Structured

SPPU practical assessment typically has two components:

1. **Term Work (TW):** Marks given by your college throughout the semester based on your lab journal, attendance in practicals, and viva performance during labs.

2. **Practical / Oral Exam:** An external or internal exam where you either perform a practical and answer related questions, or face a standalone oral (viva voce).

The split varies by subject, but a common structure is:

| Component                | Marks |
| ------------------------ | ----- |
| Term Work (TW)           | 25    |
| Practical / Oral (PR/OR) | 25    |
| Total                    | 50    |

Both TW and PR are often in addition to the theory paper's 80 marks, contributing to a subject total of 100+ in some cases. Check your specific subject's scheme.

## What Is Term Work?

Term work is evaluated **internally by your college faculty** throughout the semester. It typically includes:

- Practical journal (lab notebook with write-ups for each experiment)
- Attendance in practical sessions
- Mid-semester viva or class tests for the lab
- Assignment submissions

**The journal matters more than people think.** An examiner flipping through a messy, incomplete journal marks lower. A complete, neat journal with dated entries for every practical is worth the effort.

## The Practical / Oral Exam

For most subjects, the practical/oral exam takes place at the end of the semester alongside the theory exam session. An external examiner (from another college) often visits to conduct it, or it's held internally depending on the subject.

**What typically happens:**

- You're given a **practical problem or experiment** to perform or explain
- After performing, the examiner asks **oral questions** based on the experiment and broader subject theory
- Marks are awarded for: correct execution, understanding of what you're doing, journal, and oral answers

## What Examiners Actually Look For

Based on how SPPU practicals are structured:

**1. Understanding, not memorisation.** Examiners ask "why" questions. Why did you use this algorithm? Why does this circuit behave this way? Having a conceptual answer matters more than reciting a definition.

**2. A complete journal.** Walk in with a complete, indexed lab journal. If an examiner asks to see a specific experiment, you should be able to find it immediately.

**3. Clean execution.** Whether it's running a program, drawing a circuit, or setting up lab equipment — doing it without needing help shows competence.

**4. Confident but honest oral answers.** If you don't know something, say so and offer what you do know. Examiners dislike confident wrong answers more than honest uncertainty.

## How to Prepare in the Last Week

**Go through each experiment** in your journal and make sure you understand what it demonstrates. For each experiment, answer:

- What is the objective?
- What did you observe and why?
- What would change if you modified [key variable]?

**List the most likely oral questions** for each subject. For DBMS practicals it's usually normalization, ER diagrams, and SQL queries. For Computer Networks it's protocol working and layer functions. Pattern-based prep works here too.

**Check your journal completeness.** Every practical must have: aim, theory, procedure, observations, result, and conclusion. If any are missing, add them before the exam.

## If You're Missing Practicals

If you've missed some lab sessions, talk to your faculty before the term work is finalised. Most faculty will allow a completion window for missed practicals — but you need to ask, not assume.

Missing practicals can affect both your TW marks and your attendance. Don't let lab sessions pile up.

## Lab Exams vs Oral-Only Exams

Some subjects have a **standalone oral exam** with no practical component — common in subjects like Project, Seminar, and some Theory+Oral subjects. For these:

- Prepare a 2–3 minute explanation of your project or chosen topic
- Anticipate broad questions on the subject's core concepts
- Have your report or documentation ready to reference
